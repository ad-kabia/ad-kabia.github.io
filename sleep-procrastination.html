Why we put off sleep

My team researched how social media use before bed affects bedtime procrastination, then designed a prototype that puts a bedtime routine front and center.

Role
Interviewer, journey mapper, design strategist
Team
A. Hsu, E. Montenegro, J. Carvalho
Timeline
January 2023 – March 2023
Tools
Figma, Zoom

The problem

Bedtime procrastination (staying up later than intended even though you know you should sleep) can have detrimental effects to a person’s mental and physical health. My team and I investigated how using social media before bed influences bedtime procrastination.

Research

Our work was guided by three hypotheses we wanted to test:

  1. Customized technology interactions and algorithms cause a lack of self-regulation with technology.
  2. Technology used before bed contributes to delayed sleep.
  3. Bedtime procrastination leads to worse mental and physical health conditions.

We studied adults between 18 and 30 who reported using their phones within two hours of sleeping. We excluded night-shift workers and parents, whose bedtimes are shaped by outside demands.

  • Sleep diary. Participants logged their evening and late-night habits over two nights.
  • Interviews. Each team member conducted two interviews with participants over Zoom.
  • Analysis. We analyzed the interview transcripts and the diaries together to find emerging themes.

What we found

People finish their daytime tasks before they start getting ready for bed, which pushes back their intended bedtime.
Technology use before bed isn’t necessarily a bad habit. Many people use social media to relax, for example by watching ASMR livestreams.
“For You” feeds can trap people in endless scrolling because the content is so highly curated to them. Infinite-scroll apps like TikTok and Instagram were the most commonly used before bed.
Most people have a bedtime routine they need to complete before going to bed.
On nights when participants slept less, they felt lethargic and easily irritable.

These themes guided our journey map and our behavioral archetypes around sleep procrastination.

Design strategy

From these findings, my team and I developed a design strategy to reduce the barriers to better sleep by targeting specific opportunity areas.

Final design

We built a Figma prototype in which people prepare for sleep through the iOS Sleep settings. Our solution brings the bedtime routine to the front of the user’s attention.

  • Notification design. A notification that encourages people to read and interact with it, like the old low battery alert that users had to tap to clear. It focuses on recommended activities and relaxation techniques.
  • Opt-out defaults. For people who use the Sleep settings, some sleep-oriented decisions, such as app blockers, are on by default and can be turned off. This reduces friction and promotes healthier sleep.

Outcome and reflection

We tested the prototype with 10 users and received mostly positive feedback. Participants liked how familiar the solution felt and that it was already available on their devices. They suggested extending it to other health routines, such as exercise or meditation, and asked for contact exceptions like the ones in Do Not Disturb.

If I could redo the study, I would run a longer sleep diary study, because I’m not sure two nights were enough. I would also give the diaries more structure. Some were thin, which left less to probe in the interviews and made them harder. A diary outline or guidebook would have helped participants give more useful detail up front.
